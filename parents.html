👩‍🏫 Parents & Teachers Guide

Word Vault is a vocabulary game disguised as safe-cracking. Its sibling, Pizza Palace, trains reading — following a spec exactly. This one trains word knowledge: choosing between words that nearly mean the same thing. The difficulty lives in the language, not the tapping.

How it works in one minute

Five confusable words are shown up front on brass tiles, each tappable for its dictionary meaning on the Get Ready screen. Then sentences arrive one at a time, each with one blank. The child taps the word that fits; each correct word seats a tumbler pin, and seating them all cracks the safe (small vaults need two pins, the biggest need five). One wrong word relocks everything — that run is over, and a safe allows three runs.

  • Every tile fits the grammar. The tiles are inflected to match the sentence (marched / tiptoed / trudged / strolled / limped), so guessing by grammar is impossible; only the meaning decides.
  • A miss is the lesson. The card shows the word that fits and the word your child picked, side by side with both meanings, the cue in the sentence, and one line on the difference ("Limping means pain in one leg. This walker is exhausted all over, not hurt."). Near-synonyms are learned by contrast, and this card is where it happens.
  • Words come back. Cracked and failed safes return later as small review lockboxes with fresh sentences — three in a row to open. Spaced retrieval in new contexts is what makes vocabulary stick; a word met once is a word forgotten.
  • It adapts by itself. Clean cracks raise the level, stuck safes lower it. No settings, no timers — a slow, careful reader is never punished. A Level Up celebrates only a genuine personal best, never re-climbing after a wobble.

Under the hood: shades of meaning

Each five-word set is built like a classroom "semantic gradient", and the Get Ready screen shows it in teaching order (quiet → loud, gentle → forceful). What the game is really training:

  • Denotation vs. shading — sob, bawl and whimper all mean cry; what differs is manner and intensity. Choosing between them is precision, the skill good writers lean on.
  • Reading for the cue — the sentence always contains the evidence ("one sore paw", "boots dragging", "every step in time"). The child learns that word choice is an argument you make from the text, not a vibe.
  • The mix-ups school marks down — their / there / they're and borrow / lend aren't synonyms at all; they're the confusions that actually cost marks, and they get their own sets.
A nice follow-up conversation: pick any set the game showed and ask for the order — "put whisper, mumble, chatter, announce and bellow in a line from quietest to loudest, and tell me where mutter would go."

Why you can trust a "wrong"

The worst thing a vocabulary game can do is mark a defensible answer wrong — the child learns the game is unfair and stops trusting it. Every sentence in Word Vault is checked, before it ships, by a panel of three independent AI reviewers from two different companies, each asked to argue that some other word fits. A sentence any reviewer can defend two answers for is rewritten or thrown away. Word meanings shown on cards are real dictionary definitions (WordNet, Princeton University), shown word for word.
